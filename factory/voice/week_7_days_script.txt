Why does a week have seven days?
A day follows the Sun. A month follows the Moon. A year follows the seasons.
But nothing in the sky takes exactly seven days. So where did the week come from?
Many historians trace its roots to ancient Babylon.
Its sky-watchers tracked seven moving lights: the Sun, the Moon, and five planets easy to see by eye.
Seven fit the Moon too: its main phases come roughly every seven days.
The Jewish week, with its Sabbath, also ran on seven days.
Rome used an eight-day market week. For a while, both ran side by side, until the planet week won.
In 321 AD, Emperor Constantine made Sunday an official day off in Roman towns.
The names still carry the sky: Saturday is Saturn's day, Sunday the Sun's, Monday the Moon's.
In Spanish, martes comes from Mars, and viernes from Venus.
Not everyone liked seven. In 1793, revolutionary France tried a ten-day week, with ten-hour days.
People disliked waiting ten days to rest. The ten-day week faded, and Napoleon ended the whole calendar in 1806.
So the week isn't written in nature. It's a very old habit that stuck.
