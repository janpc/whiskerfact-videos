Goosebumps evolved for fur. So why do we get them?
Under your skin, every hair has its own tiny muscle, called the arrector pili. When it tightens, the hair stands up and the skin bumps.
First, the trigger. That muscle is wired to your sympathetic nervous system, the autopilot that reacts to cold and strong emotions.
Second, the warmth. On furry animals, raised hair traps warm air against the body, like a puffy jacket.
Third, the bluff. A frightened cat puffs up its fur and looks bigger and tougher.
Humans lost most of that fur, so the reflex is a leftover from hairier ancestors.
Here's the twist. In twenty twenty, Harvard scientists found in mice that the same nerve also reaches stem cells in the hair follicle, the root that builds each hair. In long cold spells, it wakes them up, and new hair grows.
So next time you get goosebumps, now you know. Your body is fluffing up a fur coat it lost long ago.
